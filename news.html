News

A running record of developments in parental child abduction involving Japan — law, courts, international pressure, and the families affected.

This page is updated regularly with the most significant news we can find, in English and Japanese, each item linked to its source so you can read the original. It is a summary for orientation, not legal advice.

Last updated: 2 November 2026. This revision fills in the back-months. The page had not been refreshed for a stretch, so we have gone back over the summer and added the significant developments we could verify but had not listed — newly added here are the Osaka state-liability suit of 6 July, brought by 27 separated parents, and CNN’s 11 July feature on the reform. We searched July and August in both English and Japanese; aside from these, we could verify no further parental-abduction development in those two months that was not already covered, and the standing items from September and October remain. Nothing here is padded: anything we could not confirm by date and source was left out.

23 October 2026 · Law and politics

Government tells the Diet that taking a child without the other parent’s consent “may constitute” spousal violence

At a sitting of the House of Representatives Cabinet Committee on 23 October, the member Koichiro Ichimura asked the government whether taking a child away without the consent of the other spouse is violence. The Cabinet Office official who answered — named in the report as Keiko Sawai, a bureau chief — said it depends on the individual case, but that where the taking of a child without consent is found to have had a harmful effect on mind or body, “there is a possibility it falls within violence by a spouse” as that term is defined in Article 1(1) of the Act on the Prevention of Spousal Violence. A Ministry of Justice official, named as Toshinori Takebayashi, added that if one parent moves a child without the other’s knowledge and for no particular reason, and that is judged to be domestic violence under the Act with the other parent treated as its victim, the Ministry has no objection. Three cautions. The answer is conditional — a possibility, turning on the facts — and changes no law; nothing in it makes the taking of a child an offence, and the Act it refers to is about protection orders and support measures, not about returning a child. The House’s own schedule confirms the Cabinet Committee sat that morning, but the official record of the session is not yet published, and the only account of the exchange we could find is on Chanto Kyodo Shinken Online, which reports from the separated parents’ side of this argument.

Sources: Chanto Kyodo Shinken Online (23 October 2026) · House of Representatives, sittings scheduled for 23 October 2026

14 October 2026 · Affected family

A petition over how separated parents are described: NPO Kimito asks the government to press news organisations to explain the domestic-violence support measure correctly

NPO Kimito began collecting signatures on 7 October for a petition addressed to the Minister for Internal Affairs and Communications, asking the ministry to press newspapers and broadcasters to describe the residence-concealment support measure accurately. Its argument is that when reports use the words “victim” and “perpetrator” of a measure that a municipal counter grants on application, readers take it for a finding of domestic violence, and the parent on the other side of it is treated accordingly. The release rests the case on three things: a suit brought against the town of Shiroishi in Saga prefecture by a man in his fifties from Kanagawa whose address was withheld from him as a domestic-violence perpetrator, which the town settled with an apology, the police having found no need for the measure; a 2024 survey of 509 separated parents by the organisation itself, in which 118 — about 23% — said such a measure had been taken against them; and a Nagoya High Court judgment recognising harm to personal interests where a parent could not correct the information held about them. Kimito says it approached four government-party lawmakers and had a meeting arranged with one member of the House of Representatives for 15 October. This is a campaigning organisation’s own release and its own survey; the figures are not independent. The Shiroishi case, which is corroborated by a newspaper report of the settlement, is the documented part of it.

Sources: NPO Kimito via PR TIMES (14 October 2026) · Nishinippon Shimbun, on the Shiroishi settlement and apology · Chanto Kyodo Shinken Online on the same case (10 April 2026)

14 October 2026 · Affected family

Tokyo state-liability suit: a closed session on 9 October, and plaintiffs at the courthouse press club — “even after the Civil Code was amended, the taking of children by a parent is left unchecked”

In March this year about thirty men and women whose children were taken by a spouse, and who can no longer see them, sued the state at the Tokyo District Court, arguing that the Diet’s failure to legislate against the removal of a child violated rights guaranteed by the Constitution, parental authority and custody among them; each claims ¥50,000 in damages. Kyodo reported on 7 March that the suit was about to be filed. The specialist site Chanto Kyodo Shinken Online, which follows the custody reform closely and from the separated parents’ side of it, reports that a preparatory session for oral argument was held at the Tokyo District Court on 9 October; that it was closed to the public; that four plaintiffs went to the courthouse press club the same day to describe the case, with their lead counsel Satoshi Sakka joining by telephone; and that the date of the next round of argument was still being arranged. The line in its headline — that the taking of children by a parent is left unchecked even after the amendment — is that report’s own characterisation, not anything said by the court. Two cautions: the report is partly behind a paywall and we have read only its opening, and we found no wire-service account of the session, so what is known of this case’s progress rests on a single source. It is a separate case from the suit brought at the Osaka District Court in July by twenty-seven parents.

Sources: Chanto Kyodo Shinken Online (14 October 2026, partly paywalled) · Kyodo via Ryukyu Shimpo (7 March 2026, paywalled)

9 October 2026 · Law in practice

Government tabulation: an arrangement for parent-child contact was recorded in 75.8% of April–June divorce registrations, up about 17 points

The Ministry of Justice has published its quarterly tabulation of the checkboxes on divorce registration forms, and Justice Minister Takashi Yamashita gave the headline figures at a press conference on 9 October. The quarter covered, April to June 2026, is the first full quarter under the amended Civil Code. Among divorces by mutual agreement involving minor children, 75.8% of forms recorded that the parents had settled arrangements for parent-child contact and 74.7% that they had settled child support, against 58.9% and 57.6% respectively in January–March. A third box, on sharing the day-to-day raising of the child, was added to the form in April and was ticked in 70.3% of cases. The child-support figure clears the government’s 70% target for the first time since the target was set in 2020. Two cautions. These are provisional figures. And they are boxes ticked on a form handed in at a municipal counter: they record that parents say an arrangement exists, not that a parent and child are in fact seeing each other, and nothing on the form is checked or enforced.

Sources: Ministry of Justice, tabulation of divorce-registration checkboxes (PDF, October 2026) · Ministry of Justice page · Jiji Press (9 October 2026) · Kyodo via Daily Sports (9 October 2026)

29 September 2026 · Law in practice

First national figures since the reform: joint custody for 24.7% of the children whose parents divorced in April

The Health, Labour and Welfare Ministry’s monthly vital statistics for April 2026 — preliminary figures, published on 8 September — are the first national data covering divorces registered since the amended Civil Code took effect on 1 April. Of the 12,558 minor children whose parents divorced that month, 24.7% came under the joint custody of both parents, while 8,454 of them (67.3%) came under the mother’s sole custody; the remaining 8% or so is the father’s sole custody. NHK summarised the result as “roughly a quarter.” Three cautions: this is a single month, not a trend; the figure records what was entered on divorce registrations, not whether a parent and child actually see each other; and it covers only divorces, so it says nothing about children taken while a marriage is still on the books. Since then the ministry has published its monthly report for May 2026, on 6 October, which carries the same table — minor children of divorcing parents by type of parental authority — as a reference spreadsheet beside the main tables. We have not been able to read that spreadsheet, so we give no custody figure for May; the main May tables record 13,154 divorces in the month, 6.8% fewer than in May 2025, and 73,661 in January–May.

Sources: Nikkei (29 September 2026, paywalled) · NHK (3 October 2026) · MHLW, Monthly Vital Statistics (preliminary), April 2026 · MHLW, Monthly Vital Statistics (preliminary), May 2026 (published 6 October 2026)

28 September 2026 · International

U.S. announces visa restrictions on officials who obstruct the resolution of international child-abduction cases

Secretary of State Marco Rubio announced a new policy restricting visa issuance to individuals “responsible for, or complicit in, impeding the prompt resolution of international parental child abduction cases,” and to their immediate family members. The statement names the categories it has in mind — “foreign government officials, judges, judicial administrators, law enforcement officials, and others” who intentionally obstruct these cases — and says the policy covers those who intentionally fail to implement or comply with laws, policies, or court orders to resolve cases and return children to their country of habitual residence. It is framed as “a new accountability tool to press non-compliant countries to meet their obligations,” taken under Section 212(a)(3)(C) of the Immigration and Nationality Act. Two things the statement does not do: it names no country — Japan is not mentioned — and it does not say whether the policy has been applied to any individual. Read alongside the State Department’s 2026 annual report, which did not cite Japan for a pattern of noncompliance, this is a tool created rather than a designation made.

Sources: U.S. Department of State, press statement by Secretary Marco Rubio (28 September 2026) · 2026 Annual Report on International Child Abduction (PDF)

17 September 2026 · International

Japan briefs the European Parliament’s Japan delegation in Strasbourg; its children’s-rights coordinator calls the reform a first step whose implementation must be watched

At the European Parliament’s request, Japan’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs briefed the Parliament’s Delegation for Relations with Japan in Strasbourg on 17 September, with the Ministry of Justice taking part online. The delegation’s chair Adam Bielan, twelve other members, and Vice-President Ewa Kopacz, the Parliament’s Coordinator for Children’s Rights, attended. The foreign ministry set out data on Japan’s implementation of the 1980 Hague Convention, including returns of children and the facilitation of parent-child contact; the justice ministry explained the 2024 Civil Code amendments that took effect in April 2026. Kopacz, as Japan’s own account of the meeting records her, welcomed the amendment as a first step by an important partner and said there is a need to follow how the amended law is implemented, so that joint custody and parent-child contact are secured in practice. The account of the meeting is the Japanese government’s own; we found no account of the meeting from the Parliament’s side.

Source: Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Japan, press release (18 September 2026)

11 July 2026 · International

CNN: Japan’s joint-custody reform is in force, but the taking of a child was left out of it

In a widely shared report, CNN covered the Civil Code reform that took effect in April 2026 and the parents still fighting to see their children. It notes that the reform does not make the act of taking a child an offence, and quotes a lawyer who expects parental abductions to continue because the issue was left out of the amendment. It is an international news feature, not a court ruling or new data.

Source: CNN (11 July 2026)

9 July 2026 · Affected parents

Osaka state-liability suit: 27 separated parents sue, calling the absence of any ban on taking a child “unconstitutional”

Twenty-seven men and women from seven prefectures filed suit at the Osaka District Court on 6 July, reported on 9 July, arguing that the state failed in its duty to legislate for shared parenting and adequate parent-child contact, so that the severing of the parent-child bond is unconstitutional; each claims ¥30,000 in damages. The complaint rests on the Convention on the Rights of the Child and Article 13 of the Constitution, and the suit was organised by the lawyer Masafumi Kawamura. It is a separate case from the Tokyo suit by about thirty parents filed in March. We could not verify the state’s response or any ruling.

Sources: Jiji Press (9 July 2026) · Sankei Shimbun, MBS News and Kyodo News (9 July 2026)

17 June 2026 · Affected family

A national wire feature that starts with the families: a grandmother in Kawasaki, a son who took his own life, and a grandchild she cannot see

Kyodo News published a feature on 17 June 2026, carried by regional papers including the Hokkaido Shimbun and the Kumamoto Nichinichi Shimbun, on parents and grandparents cut off from children after one parent moved out with them. Its opening, which is as far as a reader without a subscription gets, describes a 78-year-old woman in Kawasaki identified only as “A”: her eldest son died by suicide in 2024 at the age of 47, eight months after his wife took their son, who is now eleven. Photographs with the article show her holding a letter the grandchild wrote, and her son’s last note with part of the image obscured; another caption identifies Kazuki Nakamura, described as the lead plaintiff in a case, reading a letter from a child. The headline says that lawyers and municipalities may be coaching parents through the procedures involved, and reports a view that the situation is “internationally abnormal.” Three cautions. The body of the article is behind a paywall and we have read only its opening, so we cannot verify the claims the headline makes or say who holds that view. The ages, dates and the eight-month interval are the article’s own. And the piece is reporting, not a finding by any court. We add it now because the page had not carried it and because it is one of the few national-wire accounts that begins with a family rather than with the law.

Sources: Hokkaido Shimbun (17 June 2026, paywalled) · Kyodo News via Kumamoto Nichinichi Shimbun (17 June 2026, paywalled)
