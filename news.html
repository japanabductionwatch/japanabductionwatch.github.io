News

A running record of developments in parental child abduction involving Japan — law, courts, international pressure, and the families affected.

This page is updated regularly with the most significant news we can find, in English and Japanese, each item linked to its source so you can read the original. It is a summary for orientation, not legal advice.

Last updated: 12 November 2026

11 November 2026 · Affected family

“A protection order is not issued simply because it has been applied for” — a first-person account from the parent it was sought against

In a column published by Chanto Kyodo Shinken Online on 11 November, the writer Yu Sakamoto describes being handed a court summons and learning that a former spouse had applied for a protection order under the Act on the Prevention of Spousal Violence, on allegations of violence, threats and fear that the writer says do not match their own account of what happened. The column says the writer was in the end made subject to such an order and accepted it, and that it will go on to describe what followed, including the disadvantages that came in mediation and in court. The first of the eleven points it lists is that a protection order is not issued simply because one has been applied for: it is the district court, not the family court, that examines the application and decides whether the legal requirements are met, and an order to leave the family home is a court order rather than a warning. Protection orders exist to protect people from spousal violence. The column is one party’s own first-person account of their case.

Source: Chanto Kyodo Shinken Online, “What to do if a protection order is sought against you” (11 November 2026)

9 November 2026 · Affected family

Three postcards and no reply: a memoir of a grandfather who died without seeing the grandchildren

In a personal essay published by Chanto Kyodo Shinken Online on 9 November, the writer Mitsuru Munakata describes the death of his father at the age of 83 at the end of August, at home in Oita. Years earlier, after his relationship with the children’s mother broke down, his side of the family offered to take the children in — including the mother’s child from an earlier relationship — and they lived together for two years. The mother, he writes, later took the children back, saying she was the holder of parental authority, and he was separated from them. He sent the children three postcards, telling them first that his father was critically ill and then that he had died; no reply came. The only grandchild his father saw grow up, he notes, is Munakata’s own adult daughter. It is one parent’s account, published by a site that campaigns on the issue, not a court finding.

Source: Chanto Kyodo Shinken Online, “My father and his grandchildren” (9 November 2026)

2 November 2026 · Law in practice

Joint custody in agreed divorces slips from 24.7% in April to 21.5% in May, the ministry tells the Diet

At the House of Representatives Judicial Affairs Committee on 23 October, Hidehiro Mitani (Liberal Democratic Party), a former State Minister of Justice, asked for the figures on custody since the amended Civil Code took effect. Nobuhiro Matsui, director-general of the Ministry of Justice’s Civil Affairs Bureau, gave numbers drawn from the Health, Labour and Welfare Ministry’s vital statistics for divorces by mutual agreement: in April, 24.7% were registered with joint custody and 67.3% with sole custody by the mother; in May the joint-custody share fell to 21.5% and the mother’s sole custody rose to 70.1%. The figures record what parents entered on the divorce registration, not whether a parent and child are in contact. The report’s headline also cites 32 joint-custody cases decided in court in the period from April to July, 3.8%; the text we were able to read does not state what that share is of.

Source: Chanto Kyodo Shinken Online (2 November 2026, on the committee sitting of 23 October)

2 November 2026 · Law in practice

Schools that demand the resident parent’s consent before telling the other parent about their own child have no legal basis for it, a Diet exchange establishes

At the House of Representatives Judicial Affairs Committee on 30 October, Kai Kotake (Democratic Party for the People) raised the practice of school staff requiring the consent of the parent a child lives with before giving the other parent information about the child’s school life. According to Chanto Kyodo Shinken Online, the exchange established that the practice has no legal basis, and that a parent who holds parental authority may ask about their child’s school life on their own. The site’s summary also refers to the state of guidance from the education ministry.

Source: Chanto Kyodo Shinken Online (2 November 2026, on the committee sitting of 30 October)

31 October 2026 · Courts and transparency

Five training videos for family-court mediators released entirely blacked out, and raised in the House of Councillors

On 22 October, Naoto Matsumura posted on X that an information-disclosure request he had made to the Tokyo Family Court had produced its commentary videos for family mediation commissioners entirely redacted, writing that it had taken a year and four months for them to be disclosed at all. According to Chanto Kyodo Shinken Online there are five such videos, attributed to the Family Bureau of the Supreme Court’s General Secretariat and dated March 2025, and they appear to be material used in training mediation commissioners on the amended law. The site reports that Yukiko Kada (Nippon Ishin no Kai) raised the non-disclosure at the House of Councillors Committee on Judicial Affairs on 27 October, and that the reason given for withholding the content was that it might be taken as setting out a “uniform and definitive guideline.” Mediation commissioners sit on the family-court panels that handle custody and parent-child contact disputes. The article’s headline puts the reason more sharply than this; the portion we could read does not show who said that, and we were not able to confirm the sitting in the Diet’s own records.

Source: Chanto Kyodo Shinken Online (31 October 2026, on the committee sitting of 27 October)

31 October 2026 · Law and politics

Justice Minister tells the Diet that taking a child away “may in some cases constitute an unlawful act”

A week after the exchange of 23 October on spousal violence, Justice Minister Takashi Yamashita was questioned at the House of Representatives Judicial Affairs Committee on 30 October by Koichiro Ichimura (Nippon Ishin no Kai), who asked among other things whether a parent accused of domestic violence on fabricated grounds should be regarded as a victim. The minister answered that the taking of a child “may in some cases constitute an unlawful act.” Nobuhiro Matsui, director-general of the ministry’s Civil Affairs Bureau, repeated the position given on 23 October: where one parent moves a child without the other’s knowledge and for no particular reason, and that is judged to be domestic violence under the Act on the Prevention of Spousal Violence, the Ministry does not deny that the other parent is a victim under that Act. An “unlawful act” is the civil-law term for a tort under Article 709 of the Civil Code, the basis for a claim in damages rather than for a criminal charge, and a minister’s answer in committee does not change the law.

Source: Chanto Kyodo Shinken Online (31 October 2026, on the committee sitting of 30 October)

14 October 2026 · Affected family

Tokyo state-liability suit: a closed session on 9 October, and plaintiffs at the courthouse press club — “even after the Civil Code was amended, the taking of children by a parent is left unchecked”

About thirty men and women whose children were taken by a spouse, and who can no longer see them, sued the state at the Tokyo District Court in March, arguing that the Diet’s failure to legislate against the removal of a child violated constitutional rights including parental authority and custody; each claims ¥50,000 in damages. According to Chanto Kyodo Shinken Online, a preparatory session for oral argument was held at the court on 9 October behind closed doors, and four plaintiffs went to the courthouse press club the same day to describe the case, with their lead counsel Satoshi Sakka joining by telephone; the date of the next hearing was still being arranged. The plaintiffs say that even after the Civil Code was amended, the taking of children by a parent is left unchecked. It is a separate case from the suit brought at the Osaka District Court in July by twenty-seven parents.

Sources: Chanto Kyodo Shinken Online (14 October 2026) · Kyodo via Ryukyu Shimpo (7 March 2026)

28 September 2026 · International

U.S. announces visa restrictions on officials who obstruct the resolution of international child-abduction cases

Secretary of State Marco Rubio announced a new policy restricting visas for individuals “responsible for, or complicit in, impeding the prompt resolution of international parental child abduction cases,” and their immediate family members. The statement lists “foreign government officials, judges, judicial administrators, law enforcement officials, and others” who intentionally obstruct such cases or fail to implement laws, policies or court orders to resolve them and return children to their country of habitual residence, and describes the policy as a new accountability tool to press non-compliant countries to meet their obligations, taken under Section 212(a)(3)(C) of the Immigration and Nationality Act. The statement names no country, Japan among them, and does not say whether the policy has been applied to anyone; the State Department’s 2026 annual report did not cite Japan for a pattern of noncompliance.

Sources: U.S. Department of State, press statement by Secretary Marco Rubio (28 September 2026) · 2026 Annual Report on International Child Abduction (PDF)
