Mission Statement

Japan Abduction Watch.org (J.A.W.org) is a public watchdog platform dedicated to exposing the systemic failure of Japan's legal, judicial, and political institutions in protecting children from parental abduction.

Through official court records, verified testimonies, and factual documentation, we aim to:

  • Document confirmed cases of international parental child abduction involving Japan
  • Hold institutions and individuals accountable through public transparency
  • Support left-behind parents seeking the return of, or access to, their children
  • Advocate for Japan's full compliance with the Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction
All listed cases are supported by legal documents, court records, or public testimony. If you or your legal team believe you are wrongly listed, please contact us to request a formal review. See our publication & privacy policy.

Why This Matters

Japan has long been described as a “black hole” for parental child abduction. Despite acceding to the Hague Convention in 2014, enforcement remains rare, and children abducted to or within Japan are seldom returned. Left-behind parents — both Japanese and foreign — face years of separation with little institutional recourse.

Even the joint custody reform that took effect in April 2026 contains no penalty for the act of abduction itself. We believe public transparency is essential to stopping future abductions and protecting children's right to both parents.

Voices

My company sent me to the United States on an overseas assignment. My wife and our baby daughter came with me. We built a life there — a home, playdates for our daughter, a daily routine.

One day, without my knowledge or consent, my daughter's mother took her from the United States back to Japan. I later learned it had been planned in secret: she knew I had refused, and she left while I was unaware. Our daughter was still only a small child.

I did everything the law allows. I filed for my daughter's return under the Hague Convention — in Japan. The court dismissed my petition, and my appeal was rejected. The courts ruled that my daughter's “habitual residence” was Japan — not the home where she had actually been living — and so she would not be returned.

In all the time since, I have seen my daughter only once: supervised, for a short while, in a lawyers' building in Japan, because the other side required a lawyer to be present. Since that single visit, I have had no contact with her at all.

Japan is a party to the Hague Convention, yet return orders are rarely enforced, and there is still no penalty for the act of taking a child. The other side once promised, in writing, that I would be allowed to see her. That promise was never kept.

My daughter is growing up without knowing her father. I am not telling this story for revenge. I am telling it so people understand this is not a private tragedy, but a systemic one.

— A left-behind parent
